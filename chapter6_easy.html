6.1 Automatic systems

Machines that sense, decide and act — all by themselves

An automated system is a machine made of software and hardware that runs by itself, with no person needed to operate it. A human supervisor usually still keeps an eye on it from a distance, ready to step in if something goes wrong.

🍎 In plain words

Think of a self-flushing toilet or an automatic hand-dryer. Nobody presses a button — a sensor notices you, and a machine reacts.

The three-step loop

Every automated system uses the same three steps, in the same order, every time:

StepWhat it does
1. SensorTakes a reading from the world around it (temperature, light, pressure…)
2. MicroprocessorCompares that reading with a stored value and decides what to do
3. ActuatorCarries out the physical action — moving a motor, opening a valve
🍎 In plain words

Sensor = eyes and ears. Microprocessor = brain. Actuator = hands and feet. If a reading is analogue (like a smooth temperature change), a device called an ADC turns it into digital numbers first, because the "brain" only understands digital data.

🧮 Example — an automatic drinks bottling plant
1Sensors check the right amount of each ingredient has gone into the mixing tank.
2A stirrer (actuator) automatically mixes it.
3Sensors check each bottle gets the right amount of drink and gas.
➜ The whole thing — from mixing to labelling — happens with no human touching it.

Automatic systems are everywhere

🏭
Industry
A nuclear power station's computer keeps everything at a safe temperature and pressure.
🚗
Transport
Self-parking cars use sensors to steer themselves into a space.
🌾
Agriculture
Sensors in the soil tell the system when to turn a water pump on.
🌦️
Weather
A weather station measures temperature, wind, rain and daylight automatically.

Good and bad points

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Much faster than a person; safer for humans; runs 24 hours a dayExpensive to build and test; can still be hacked; needs a lot of maintenance
⚠️ If a question asks for an advantage to the employee, don't write things that actually help the employer, like "the robot never needs a break" — that only helps the company, not the worker.

6.2 Robots

What actually makes something a "robot"?

Robotics is the branch of science about designing, building and running robots. The word "robot" comes from a Czech word meaning "forced labour".

🍎 In plain words

Not every machine is a robot — a toaster isn't a robot, but a robot vacuum cleaner is. What's the difference? Read on.

Robots show up in three places

🏭
Factories
Welding, spray-painting, and warehouse robots.
🏠
At home
Robot floor-sweepers and lawn mowers.
🚁
Drones
Flying robots for photos or deliveries.

The three things every robot needs

CharacteristicWhat it means
1. Can senseUses sensors to notice light, pressure, temperature, etc.
2. Can moveHas a body (wheels, arms, motors) that can move or grip
3. Is programmableHas a "brain" (controller) that can be given instructions
⚠️ A robot does not need artificial intelligence (AI) to be a robot! Most robots just repeat the same task over and over — that's not the same as being "intelligent".
🍎 In plain words

A chatbot that talks to you on a website is not a real robot — it has no body, so it can't sense or move in the physical world. It's just a program.

Two kinds of robot

Independent (autonomous)Dependent
No human controls it — it works completely on its ownA human directly controls or guides it
🧮 Example — a doctor controls a surgery robot remotely
1Good: a specialist doctor anywhere in the world can operate on the patient.
2Good: smaller cuts can mean a faster recovery.
3Bad: if the internet connection drops, the operation might have to stop.
➜ Careful — since a human is controlling the robot, "the robot never gets tired" is NOT a good answer here!

6.3 Artificial intelligence (AI)

Getting computers to act a bit more like humans

Artificial intelligence means getting a computer to copy intelligent human behaviour — the kind of thinking humans do using thought, experience and their senses.

🍎 In plain words

A normal computer program only ever does exactly what it's told. An AI system can also learn from experience and handle situations nobody programmed it for — a bit like how you learn to ride a bike by practising, not by reading an instruction manual.

Three levels of AI compared to a human

TypeHow good compared to a human
Narrow AIBetter than a human at ONE task
General AIAbout the same as a human at a task
Strong AIBetter than a human at MANY tasks
🧮 Example — teaching an AI to make the perfect cup of tea
1The AI tries different water temperatures and steeping times, and learns which combination works best.
2It builds its own set of rules from what it learned.
3Next time it's asked to make coffee, it reuses and tweaks those same rules instead of starting from zero.
➜ That's learning and adapting — two key things AI can do that a normal program can't.

Everyday AI

🔊
Smart speakers
Alexa or Siri listen and get better at understanding you over time.
💬
Chatbots
Copy the pattern of a human conversation, in text or voice.
🙂
Face recognition
Looks at your eyebrows and mouth to guess your emotion.

Expert systems — a computer that acts like a specialist

An expert system asks you questions and gives you an expert answer, like a doctor would. It's built from four parts:

PartJob
User interfaceAsks you the questions (usually Yes/No)
Inference engineCompares your answers to the facts and works out the answer
Knowledge baseAll the stored facts
Rules baseThe "IF this, THEN that" rules
🧮 Example — guessing an animal
1Is it a mammal? Yes. Can it be a pet? No.
2Does it live in water? Yes. Does it have legs? No.
➜ Conclusion: it's a WHALE!
⚠️ If asked "how does the expert system decide?", don't just list the four parts — explain HOW they work together: the inference engine checks your answers against the knowledge base, then follows the rules base to reach a conclusion.

Machine learning

Machine learning is when a computer gets better at a task just by looking at lots of examples — without a person rewriting its code.

🍎 In plain words

Think of a spam filter. Nobody programs in every possible spam email — instead, the computer looks at thousands of past emails marked "spam" or "not spam" and learns the pattern itself.
