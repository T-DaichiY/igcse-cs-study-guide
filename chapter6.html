6.1 Automated Systems

Sensors, microprocessors and actuators working together with no human intervention

An automated system is a combination of software and hardware — sensors, microprocessors and actuators — designed and programmed to work automatically without the need for any human intervention. Such systems still often involve human monitoring: a supervisor typically watches the process remotely and can override it if something goes wrong.

The sensor → microprocessor → actuator loop

Every automated system, however complicated it looks at first, is built from the same three-part relationship:

StageComponentRole
1. InputSensor(s)Takes a reading from the environment (temperature, pressure, light…). Analogue readings are converted to digital by an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), since a microprocessor can only process digital data.
2. ProcessMicroprocessor / computerCompares the sensor data with pre-set/stored values or programmed rules and decides what action (if any) is needed.
3. OutputActuator(s)Receives a signal from the microprocessor and performs the physical task — turning a motor, opening a valve, applying a brake.
💡 How to answer any "describe the automated system" question: work through the loop in order — name the specific sensor(s) relevant to the scenario, state what data they collect, say the (analogue) data may need an ADC, say it is sent to the microprocessor which compares it with a pre-set/stored value, and finally say the microprocessor sends a signal to a named actuator to perform a specific physical action. A vague answer that just says "sensors detect things and actuators respond" without naming the sensor, the comparison, and the actuator's specific action will lose marks.
🧮 Worked example — a fully automatic bottling plant for fizzy drinks
1Sensors check that the correct amount of each ingredient has been added to the mixing tank.
2A stirrer (an actuator) is automatically activated whenever ingredients are added, to mix them.
3At the bottling stage, sensors check the correct amount of drink and carbon dioxide gas has been added to each bottle.
4The central computer receives all sensor data continuously, compares it against the pre-set recipe values, and sends signals to actuators (valves, the stirrer motor, the capping mechanism) to control mixing, bottling, gas injection, capping and labelling.
➜ The whole process — from mixing through to labelling — runs with no human intervention.

Automated systems across different sectors

The syllabus expects you to recognise the sensor → microprocessor → actuator loop in scenarios drawn from industry, transport, agriculture, weather, gaming, lighting and science:

🏭
Industry
A nuclear power station's distributed control system (DCS) uses temperature, pressure, flow and radiation sensors, with actuators on pumps, valves and an emergency shut-down mechanism.
🚗
Transport
Self-parking cars use bumper sensors and cameras to gauge a space, then actuators control the steering rack, brakes and throttle.
🌾
Agriculture
An automated irrigation system uses ultrasonic water-level sensors and a wireless link to a controller, which starts/stops pumps via actuators.
🌦️
Weather
An automated weather station uses a thermometer, anemometer, hygrometer, barometer, level sensor and light sensor, feeding a microprocessor.
🎮
Gaming
Accelerometers detect tilting; proximity sensors in smart touch pads detect finger position — both feed a microcontroller for immersive gameplay.
💡
Lighting
A light sensor detects darkness and the microprocessor signals actuators to control garden LEDs and a fountain's water pumps.
🧪
Science
In a pharmaceutical process, level sensors and a colorimeter feed a microprocessor, which opens/closes a burette tap via an actuator.

Worked example — advantages and disadvantages of a nuclear power station's automated system

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Much faster than a human operator at taking necessary actionExpensive to set up in the first place, and needs considerable testing
Safer — more likely to make timely interventions than a human, and keeps humans away from a dangerous environmentAlways possible for a set of conditions to occur that were never considered during testing — hence the need for a human supervisor
The process runs closer to optimum conditions, since small changes are identified and corrected very quicklySubject to cyberattacks no matter how good the system (a countermeasure is having no external links, though the connection to the supervisor is still a potential weak link)
Less expensive in the long run, since an automated system replaces most of the 24-hour monitoring workforceNeeds enhanced maintenance, which can be expensive
⚠️ Common misconception (confirmed in two separate June 2025 Examiner Reports, Papers 0478/11 and 0478/13): when a question asks specifically for an advantage/disadvantage to a named party (e.g. "to the employee" or "to the worker"), many candidates repeatedly wrote generic advantages that actually benefit the employer instead (e.g. "robots don't need breaks", "robots work 24/7") — these earn zero marks because they don't answer the question that was actually asked. Always re-read exactly who the advantage/disadvantage must apply to before answering.
📌 Increased automation in industry has improved quality dramatically: in manual manufacture of car engines, piston installation had an error rate of around 1.5%; with automated systems this fell to about 0.00001%.

6.2 Robotics

What makes a machine a robot, and the roles robots play across industry, transport, agriculture, medicine, the home and entertainment

Robotics is a branch of (computer) science that brings together the design, construction and operation of robots. The word "robot" comes from the Czech word robota, meaning "forced labour", first used in the 1920s play Rossum's Universal Robots.

💡 Command-word discipline: a full-mark definition of robotics names it as a branch of (computer) science and includes all three of design, construction and operation — dropping any one of the three, or describing robotics only as "building robots", loses marks.

Robots across the syllabus's three named categories

🏭
Factory equipment
Welding, spray-painting, fitting windscreens, cutting metal parts, bottling/labelling plants, automatic warehouses.
🏠
Domestic robots
Autonomous floor sweepers, lawn mowers, pool cleaners, window cleaners and "friend" entertainment robots.
🚁
Drones
Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), remotely controlled or autonomous, used for reconnaissance or parcel delivery.

The three defining characteristics of a robot

To correctly be called a robot, a machine must demonstrate all three of the following:

CharacteristicDetail
1. Ability to sense its surroundingsVia sensors (light, pressure, temperature, acoustic…) that let it recognise its environment. Data is sent to a microprocessor or computer.
2. A degree of movementA mechanical structure/framework (wheels, cogs, pistons, gears) plus electrical components (motors, actuators, circuit boards). Robots can use end-effectors — attachments for tasks like welding, spraying or lifting.
3. ProgrammableA "brain" — the controller — determines the action to take, relying on data from sensors/cameras. Controllers are programmable, which lets a robot carry out tasks automatically.
⚠️ Common misconception, confirmed in the June 2025 Examiner Report (Paper 0478/11, Question 8(a)): "Some candidates gave an inaccurate response of 'artificial intelligence'. It would be beneficial for candidates to understand that not all robots have artificial intelligence. It is not a key characteristic of a robot and does not define a robot." Many robots simply carry out repetitive tasks and need no adaptive, "intelligent" behaviour at all.

It's also important not to confuse physical robots with software robots — a search-engine WebCrawler that scans websites, or a chatbot that appears to hold a conversation — which do not meet the physical definition of a robot at all.

Independent vs dependent robots

Independent robotDependent robot
No direct human control — described as autonomous (e.g. an autonomous vehicle)A human interfaces directly with it, via a computer or control panel
Can replace human activity totally; no human interaction is required to functionCan only supplement, not totally replace, human activity — e.g. humans and robots working together on a car assembly line

Roles of robots across sectors

SectorExample role
IndustryPaint spraying, welding, manufacturing microchips — capable of working in hazardous conditions, 24/7, but can struggle with "non-standard" tasks
TransportAutonomous cars, buses, trains and (in future) airplanes use sensors, cameras and actuators to sense and react to conditions safely
AgricultureHarvesting robots (e.g. the "vegebot") pick only ripe produce; weed-control robots use AI and GPS to distinguish crop from weed
MedicineSurgical robots, blood-sampling robots, microbots for targeted therapy, and robotic prosthetic limbs
DomesticAutonomous vacuum cleaners and grass cutters use proximity sensors/cameras to avoid obstacles and cover a whole area
EntertainmentTheme-park character robots, camera-operating robots, and robots that synchronise lighting/effects to within a millisecond
🧮 Worked example — a real 0478 exam question on a human-controlled surgery robot (June 2025, Paper 0478/12, Q5)

Scenario: a doctor controls a surgical robot remotely to operate on a patient.

1Electrical components asked for: any two of sensors, microprocessors, actuators.
2Advantages (point + expansion): "a doctor doesn't need to travel… so any specialist in the world can operate"; "surgery enhances precision/accuracy… so a smaller incision can be made… so recovery time may be shorter"; "components can be smaller than a human hand"; "surgery is safer/more hygienic… keeps the doctor away from an infectious patient".
3Disadvantages (point + expansion): "the internet connection could be lost/delayed… so surgery may not be able to continue"; "the robot could be hacked… and endanger the patient's life".
➜ The examiner report notes most candidates lost marks in part (ii) by giving generic reasons like "robots don't need breaks" — but the scenario states a human is controlling the robot, so that generic reason does not apply here.
📌 Autonomous cars use radar, ultrasonic sensors and cameras as their "eyes and ears"; research shows autonomous vehicles could increase lane capacity by up to +100% and average speed by +20%, while removing human error from driving decisions.

6.3 Artificial Intelligence

Simulating intelligent human behaviour — reasoning, learning, expert systems and machine learning

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science dealing with the simulation of intelligent human behaviour by a computer — often described as replicating the cognitive functions of the human brain (the mental process of acquiring knowledge through thought, experience and the five senses).

💡 Command-word discipline: a full-mark definition of AI needs two parts — (1) that it is a branch of computer science, and (2) that its purpose is the simulation of intelligent human behaviour by a computer. "A computer that thinks" or "when a machine is smart" won't gain full credit — it omits the technical framing the mark scheme expects.

How AI differs from a normal computer program

Standard computer programAI system
Only does exactly what it is coded to doCan learn from new data and adapt its behaviour to new situations
Cannot handle a situation that was never explicitly programmedCan reason and generalise to situations it has not seen before
Requires a human to rewrite the code for new requirementsCan change its own behaviour without being explicitly reprogrammed

Three pillars of AI, and three levels compared with humans

AI is essentially a collection of rules and data, combined with the ability to reason, learn and adapt to external stimuli:

CategoryDefinition
Narrow AIA machine has superior performance to a human doing one specific task
General AIA machine is similar (not superior) to a human's performance on a specific task
Strong AIA machine has superior performance to a human in many tasks
🧮 Worked example — deductive reasoning (making the perfect cup of tea)
1AI works out how to make the perfect cup of tea, based on facts (water temperature, steeping time, milk quantity).
2The machine learns from this experience and builds a set of rules from it.
3Next time it's asked to make a related drink (coffee, hot chocolate), it applies and modifies its learned rules rather than starting from nothing.
➜ Over time the AI performs the task more effectively, even applying its rules to a completely new situation.
📰
News generation
Processes live news feeds continuously to generate content.
🔊
Smart home devices
Alexa, Siri, Cortana — recognise voice commands and become more sophisticated over time.
💬
Chatbots
Replicate patterns of human interaction to respond to typed or voice messages.
🙂
Facial expression recognition
Maps combinations of facial landmarks (eyebrows, mouth corners) to emotions.

Expert systems

An expert system uses AI to simulate the judgement and behaviour of a human expert — solving problems or answering questions that would normally require one. The syllabus limits this to exactly four required components:

ComponentRole
User interfaceHow the system interacts with the user — dialogue boxes, command prompts; usually Yes/No questions based on previous answers
Inference engineThe main processing element — acts like a search engine, examining the knowledge base for matches and applying the rules base to reach a conclusion
Knowledge baseA repository of facts — a collection of objects and their attributes, gathered from expert resources
Rules baseA set of inference rules used by the inference engine, usually IF statements, e.g. IF continent = "South America" AND language = "Portuguese" THEN country = "Brazil"
🧮 Worked example — how a knowledge base + rules base reach a conclusion
1Is it a mammal? YES
2Can it be a pet? NO
3Does it live in water? YES · Does it make sonic sounds? YES · Is its body covered in skin? YES · Does it have legs? NO
➜ Conclusion: it is a WHALE.
⚠️ Common misconception, confirmed in the June 2025 Examiner Report (Paper 0478/12, Question 5(a)): when asked to describe how an expert system decides a diagnosis, most candidates only listed and defined the component parts rather than explaining the process. "Candidates found this question challenging… Few candidates were able to relate this to the context given and did not describe how the components are used to decide a diagnosis." Simply naming and defining the four components is not enough — describe the process: the inference engine compares input to the knowledge base, then applies the rules base to reach a conclusion.

Machine learning

Machine learning is the science of training computers with sample data so they can make predictions about new, unseen data, without needing to be specifically reprogrammed. It is a sub-set of AI.

AIMachine learning
Represents simulated intelligence in machinesGetting machines to make decisions without being explicitly programmed to do so
Aims to build machines capable of thinking like humansAims to make machines that learn through data acquisition, to solve new problems

A general machine learning model development cycle: 1. Data collection → 2. Data cleaning → 3. Exploration and analysis → 4. Building a model → 5. Evaluation. This applies to spam-email filtering, collaborative-filtering shopping recommendations, and fraud detection alike.

📌 Deep learning (beyond the 0478 syllabus, for interest) is a sub-set of machine learning that arranges algorithms in layers as an artificial neural network. It needs large amounts of data and computing power, and is especially good at complex visual tasks — but its reasoning is often called a "black box" since it's hard to see why a decision was made.

🔑 Key terms

Automated system
Software + hardware designed to work automatically with no human intervention.
Sensor / ADC
Input device reading the environment / converts analogue readings to digital.
Microprocessor / Actuator
Decides the action from sensor data / physically performs it.
Distributed control system (DCS)
A powerful computer that monitors/controls a complex process with no human interaction.
Robotics
The branch of (computer) science covering the design, construction and operation of robots.
Autonomous
Able to operate independently, with no human input required.
End-effector
An attachment on a robot arm for a specific task, e.g. spray painting.
Artificial intelligence (AI)
Branch of computer science simulating intelligent human behaviour.
Narrow / General / Strong AI
Superior at one task / similar to a human / superior at many tasks.
Expert system
AI mimicking a human expert's judgement, via a knowledge base, rules base, inference engine and interface.
Inference engine
Compares input to the knowledge base and applies the rules base to reach a conclusion.
Machine learning
A sub-set of AI that trains on data to make predictions without being reprogrammed.

📌 Exam tips

If a question asks for an advantage/disadvantage to a named party (employee, patient…), only give points that genuinely benefit or harm that specific party — not the employer.

When a scenario says a human is controlling the robot (e.g. remote surgery), don't use generic "robots don't need breaks" answers — they don't apply to a human-controlled robot.

For expert-system questions, describe the process (inference engine compares input to the knowledge base, then applies the rules base) — don't just define the four components.
